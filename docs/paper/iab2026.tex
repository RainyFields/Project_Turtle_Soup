\begin{abstract}
Interactive agents should use feedback to converge on correct answers, yet how
they search remains poorly understood. We study three Qwen model tiers on 22
Turtle Soup puzzles using scored answer checkpoints and embedded trajectories
of successive questions. Accuracy improves for only the first 10 of 30 rounds,
while semantic stride contracts four- to fivefold. Longer budgets, Oracle
quality, and puzzle under-determination do not explain this plateau. Feedback
also redirects search poorly: after refutations, all three tiers move toward
regions already ruled out. The tiers also fail differently underneath the shared
plateau. The smallest circles a single early hypothesis; the largest reaches
solution-grade stories and then abandons them. Endpoint accuracy cannot
distinguish these two failures. We release the evaluation harness,
process measures, verified puzzle set, and per-game logs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider a Turtle Soup puzzle: \emph{A man's body lies in the desert,
clutching half a matchstick; luggage and clothing are scattered around him.}
The solver sees only this surface and must recover the hidden explanation
through yes/no questions. In the solution, a hot-air balloon was losing
altitude. After discarding their luggage, the passengers drew lots, and the
man holding the short matchstick was thrown overboard.

Existing evaluations~\cite{turtlebench,splat,tsbench} can determine whether an agent eventually recovers this
story, but not how it searches. The same endpoint score may describe an agent
that never leaves its initial hypothesis and one that discovers the answer but
later abandons it. 
Existing Turtle Soup benchmarks emphasize outcomes rather
than search dynamics (Appendix~\ref{app:rw}).
This leaves a central question unresolved: how does feedback change an agent's
next move?

Turtle Soup provides a controlled setting for answering this question. The
interaction is textual, the Oracle supplies a discrete yes/no/irrelevant
signal, and each puzzle is deliberately under-determined. We introduce two
process-level probes. After every Oracle response, the Questioner produces a
complete checkpoint reconstruction, allowing accuracy to be tracked across
rounds. We also embed each question to trace the association trajectory and
measure its per-round semantic stride. We evaluate three Qwen model tiers on
22 human-played puzzles.

We test three hypotheses. \textbf{H1}: refutations progressively constrain
the hypothesis space, so shorter strides should accompany convergence.
\textbf{H2}: feedback redirects the next question, with refutations pushing
the search away and confirmations promoting local refinement. \textbf{H3}:
once an agent reaches a solution-grade reconstruction, it preserves and
commits that answer.

Our analysis shows that interactive search can narrow without converging. It
also separates two failures hidden by endpoint accuracy: circling around an
early hypothesis and abandoning a stronger earlier reconstruction. Finally,
we establish an important measurement boundary: embedding geometry can
describe these trajectories but cannot reliably score their success. We
release the evaluation harness, process instruments, verified puzzle set, and
per-game logs.

\section{Framework and experiments}\label{sec:framework}

\paragraph{Task and harness.}
A \textbf{Questioner} sees only the puzzle's visible \emph{surface} and asks
one yes/no question per round, following the SPLAT player--judge
protocol~\cite{splat}. An \textbf{Oracle} holds the hidden story and answers
yes, no, or irrelevant. The Questioner may submit a final story at any round
and must do so when the round cap is reached — the maximum number of rounds a game may run.

\paragraph{Checkpoint and commitment instrumentation.}
After every Oracle response, the Questioner writes its best complete reconstruction. We score each checkpoint, so every point on the accuracy
curve represents a full answer based on the evidence available at that round.
We separately record \emph{commitment}, defined by whether and when the
Questioner voluntarily submits a final story. Together, these measures
convert an endpoint benchmark into a scored process.

\paragraph{Puzzles.}
To ensure that the intended explanations are demonstrably recoverable, we
restrict evaluation to puzzles with a public record of human play. The
resulting set contains 22 puzzles from a Chinese Turtle Soup community site,
with 97 annotated key clues. Each puzzle receives an \textbf{under-determination} score: one minus the best of twelve cold guesses made from the surface alone. It predicts what a model writes before any evidence arrives. Appendix~\ref{app:set} gives the distribution and validation.



\paragraph{Outcome scoring.}
The outcome score combines \textbf{clue recall} (70 points) and
\textbf{causal-logic identity} (30 points), as detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}. The logic component recognizes causally
equivalent answers expressed through different wording. The clue component
anchors answers to the annotated evidence and prevents semantically distant
alternatives from receiving high scores.

\paragraph{Association-trajectory instrumentation.}\label{sec:traj}
Our second instrument analyzes the questions themselves, adapting forward
flow~\cite{forwardflow} and the Divergent Association Task~\cite{dat} from
human psychometrics. For each round, we extract keywords with jieba (a standard Chinese word segmenter), embed
them using bge-small-zh-v1.5, and average the normalized embeddings into a
unit vector $q_t$. The same extractor is used for every model, so no model is measured through its own vocabulary. All distances are cosine distances.

We use two anchors. The surface-only anchor represents terms from the visible
puzzle surface, whereas the solution-aware anchor combines surface, solution,
and clue terms. The primary measures are the \textbf{stride},
$s_t=d(q_t,q_{t-1})$, and the \textbf{anchor distance},
$h_t=d(q_t,\mathcal{A})$, computed separately for each anchor. H1 predicts
that contraction in stride should accompany movement toward the solution and
improved checkpoint accuracy. H2 is tested by pairing each Oracle response at
round $t$ with the next stride $s_{t+1}$ and by comparing movement toward
previously confirmed and refuted regions. H3 is tested by comparing
\emph{solution-grade} checkpoint peaks --- those reaching $0.5$ and held
across two consecutive rounds, so that a single lucky wording does not
qualify --- with subsequent commitment and final answers.

\paragraph{Experimental designs.}
E1 plays each game for up to 30 rounds and scores a checkpoint after every
round. E2 imposes hard caps of $\{5,10,15,20,25,30\}$ rounds and scores only
the forced final answer. E3 analyzes the
E1 trajectories using $\{s_t\}$, $\{h_t\}$, response-conditioned movement,
and checkpoint commitment.

\paragraph{Model grid and validity controls.}
We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B on 22 puzzles with
three seeds. E1 therefore contains 198 thirty-round games, while E2 contains
1,188 games across the six caps. DeepSeek-V3.1 serves as both Oracle and
causal-logic judge. It is cross-family with every Questioner and achieves
95\% accuracy on held-out yes/no probes. Every model call uses
\texttt{max\_tokens}=2048, disabled thinking, temperature 0.2, and a
50,000-token per-game budget. 
The smallest tier sometimes exhausts its per-game token budget and is then forced to answer. We report those games separately throughout, so that a harness limit is not read as agent behaviour.
The three Questioners differ in more than size: 27B is a later generation, and 397B is a mixture-of-experts with 17B active parameters. We therefore compare model tiers and do not claim a scaling law.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Interaction improves accuracy only during the first ten rounds.}
Checkpoint accuracy increased through approximately round 10 and then
plateaued at every tier (Figure~\ref{fig:e1fig}a). From rounds 10 to 30,
accuracy changed by $-0.009$, $+0.003$, and $-0.013$ for 4B, 27B, and 397B,
respectively; all confidence intervals included zero. Semantic stride
contracted monotonically over the same rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:e1fig}b): the
search narrowed while accuracy did not move.

The plateau was not explained by the round cap. Final accuracy was insensitive
to it for the two larger tiers, and the apparent decline at 4B tracked token
exhaustion (0/66 to 63/66 games across caps) rather than the cap itself. The Oracle answered 95\% of held-out probes correctly, with 68--74\%
refusals, so the feedback channel had not collapsed. Nor did puzzle
under-determination explain it: improvement after round 10 was unrelated to
how open a puzzle is, at every tier ($|\rho| \leq 0.10$, n.s.\ at $n=22$).

\paragraph{H1: does the search narrow toward the answer?}
It narrows, but not toward the answer. Mean stride decreased monotonically at every tier, falling four- to fivefold
from $0.145/0.232/0.182$ at round 1 to $0.025/0.053/0.045$ at round 29. This
contraction did not accompany convergence: within-tier stride did not predict
final performance ($|\rho|\leq0.16$). The
4B tier had the smallest late-game stride, $0.030$ $[0.021,0.039]$, compared
with $0.082$ $[0.065,0.098]$ for 27B, yet never reached a solution-grade peak. Its trajectory therefore contracted around a persistent hypothesis
rather than toward the solution, the pattern we call \emph{circling}.

\paragraph{H2: does the answer redirect the next question?}
Not in the direction predicted, and at the smallest tier not at all.
We paired each Oracle response with the next stride and controlled for round
position within puzzle and round-bin cells. Let $\Delta s$ denote stride
after confirmation minus stride after refutation. No difference was detected
for 4B ($+0.001$ $[-0.009,+0.011]$), whereas 27B and 397B moved farther after
confirmation ($+0.028$ $[+0.010,+0.046]$ and $+0.009$ $[+0.002,+0.017]$;
Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}a). Response-conditioned movement was therefore
absent at the smallest tier and opposite to the hypothesized direction at the
larger tiers. The control matters: confirmations arrive earlier than
refutations and early strides are larger, so without it an apparent effect
appears at every tier (Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} reports both estimates).

A confirmation is the rarer answer and could reasonably justify a larger step,
so we also ran a test that assumes nothing about the right step size: after
each response, is the next question nearer to regions the Oracle has already
refuted, or to those it has already confirmed? After a refutation every tier
moved toward the refuted side: $-0.089$ $[-0.112,-0.068]$ for 4B, $-0.051$
$[-0.075,-0.032]$ for 27B, and $-0.032$ $[-0.047,-0.018]$ for 397B. After a
confirmation no such asymmetry appeared, although refutations outnumber
confirmations roughly three to one in both comparisons, so the effect is not
an artifact of the larger set. Feedback
therefore failed to steer any tier away from rejected regions.

\paragraph{H3: does the agent stop when it finds the answer?}
Rarely, and least often at the tier that found the most. The 4B tier reached no solution-grade checkpoint. The
27B tier reached solution-grade checkpoints in four games and voluntarily
committed in three; 397B did so in six games but committed in only one
(Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}b). Commitment grew rarer and later with
scale: 11/66, 9/66, and 2/66 games at mean rounds 11.1, 14.2, and 23.5 for
4B, 27B, and 397B. The largest tier therefore reached useful hypotheses most
often and preserved them least. The next paragraph measures that failure on a
looser threshold that admits near-misses: \emph{abandoning} games are those
whose best checkpoint reaches $0.3$ and whose final falls below half of it
($n=34$).

\paragraph{Embedding geometry describes abandonment but does not score it.}
In abandoning games, the median cosine similarity between the final and peak
stories was $0.851$. This was comparable to the similarity between
checkpoints from the same game ($0.841$) and much greater than that between
stories from different puzzles ($0.498$; Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}c).
Despite this semantic proximity, the median score across the same
peak-to-final pairs falls from $0.447$ to $0.099$. The decisive substitution
was therefore smaller than the
encoder's resolution, which is why the trajectory geometry characterized how
agents failed without predicting how well they scored
(Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

\textbf{Limitations.}
Our evidence comes from one game, one Oracle protocol, and three Qwen-family
Questioners. The trajectory measures capture how far each question moves in
embedding space, not what operation it performs; round position is
controlled only by binning, the anchors are not human-derived, and the
encoder's resolution is itself a limit on what any embedding-based instrument
can score (\S\ref{sec:results}). Token
exhaustion can mimic inability to form questions; the Oracle's 95\%
audited accuracy implies roughly one wrong response per game; and using the
same DeepSeek model as Oracle and logic judge may correlate their errors.
Under-determination may partly measure puzzle familiarity, though no claim
rests on it. The puzzles include death and other dark themes and
carry content flags.

\textbf{Conclusion.}
The agents narrow without converging:
semantic stride decreases four- to fivefold while accuracy plateaus, and
feedback fails to steer questions consistently away from rejected regions.
The smallest tier mostly circles early hypotheses, whereas the largest tier
sometimes reaches solution-grade reconstructions but fails to preserve or
commit them. The two failures require different fixes yet appear
identical under endpoint accuracy; process-level measurement reveals how
search fails even when it cannot predict performance. Whether these patterns extend to other tasks, model families, and protocols
remains open.

\section{Related work}\label{app:rw}
Existing Turtle Soup benchmarks score outcomes. TurtleBench~\cite{turtlebench},
SPLAT~\cite{splat} (whose interactive player--judge protocol we adopt), and
TurtleSoup-Bench~\cite{tsbench} evaluate verification accuracy, solve
efficiency, or faithfulness to the intended story. TurtleSoup-Bench also
probes what agents ask next, but it grades the question against the intended
story; none of the three measures the geometry of the search itself, which is
the gap this paper addresses. MUTATE~\cite{mutate} shows that frontier agents
find far fewer mechanism-distinct solution paths than humans; its
diverge-then-narrow agent is a natural intervention to test with our
instruments. The trajectory instrument adapts forward
flow~\cite{forwardflow} and the Divergent Association Task~\cite{dat} from
human psychometrics, and the Small World of Words norms~\cite{swow} are the
natural source for the human-derived anchor that ours is not yet
(\S\ref{sec:traj}). Catalogues of judge bias~\cite{calm,posbias} motivated
confining the model judge to the one subscore that objective matching cannot
reach (Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\section{The puzzle set}\label{app:set}

\paragraph{Selection.} The 22 puzzles were hand-picked from the classic
repertoire of a Chinese Turtle Soup community, restricted to puzzles with a
public record of human play (\S\ref{sec:framework}): a puzzle nobody has
solved carries no evidence that its clues suffice or that the intended leap
is reachable. Every candidate was read and kept or rejected by hand.
Surfaces run 9--108 characters (median 34) and solutions 13--200 characters
(median 102).

\paragraph{Distribution of under-determination.} Under-determination is
defined in \S\ref{sec:framework}. Its distribution over the 22 puzzles is:

\begin{center}\small

\toprule
\textbf{Under-determination} & \textbf{Puzzles} \\
\midrule
0.15--0.25 & 5 \\
0.25--0.35 & 8 \\
0.35--0.45 & 4 \\
0.45--0.55 & 4 \\
0.55--0.65 & 1 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The median is 0.347, the quartiles are 0.273 and 0.410, and the full range
is 0.173--0.577: single-peaked with a thin hard tail. The easiest puzzles are
the story the genre is named for (0.17) and the desert matchstick (0.19); the
hardest is a fourteen-character surface reading \emph{our heights differ / a
flowerpot broke / our heights are the same} (0.58).

\paragraph{Validity as a covariate.} The score describes the puzzle, not any
agent's performance on it, and it is independent of rounds used; a difficulty
defined as ``how many rounds this takes'' would make an accuracy-versus-rounds
plot confirm itself. It is not a proxy for surface length ($r=-0.22$, n.s.\ at
$n=22$, a weak test). It is also one measure rather than two: a surface loose
enough to admit many mechanisms is, for the same reason, one whose true
mechanism takes more reframing to reach, so \emph{how far} and \emph{how many
ways} are not separable here.

\paragraph{Familiarity caveat.} The two puzzles scoring easiest are among the
most widely circulated, and the models' first-round guesses are consistent
with familiarity contributing: on the best-known of them, the two larger
tiers open at 0.61 and 0.53 before any evidence arrives, against 0.24 and
0.36 on a puzzle of almost identical under-determination. On the easy end the
measure may therefore read familiarity as much as difficulty. Separating the
two would take a memorisation probe, which we have not run; no claim in the
paper rests on the measure.

\section{Measuring agent performance}\label{app:metrics}

\paragraph{Outcome score.} The outcome score combines \textbf{clue recall
(70 points)} and \textbf{causal-logic identity (30 points)}
(\S\ref{sec:framework}). Clue recall matches the answer against per-puzzle
annotated key clues and is objective and reproducible; matching tolerates
paraphrase through character-bigram recall, which sets a floor on how short
an annotated clue may be (below the floor only a verbatim match counts, so
all clues are annotated above it). The logic judge rates only whether cause
$\to$ mechanism $\to$ outcome matches and is instructed to ignore wording.
Single ratings are unstable on borderline answers, so each rating is sampled
twice at temperature 0.2 and averaged, which is enough to make the judged half
reproducible: the two samples agree exactly in 86\% of ratings and differ by
under one point of 30 on average.

\paragraph{Causal logic needs its own score.} Models reach the right causal
shape before, and often without, landing the annotated clue content. Every
tier therefore earns proportionally far more of the judged causal-logic half
than of the clue-recall half --- three- to fourfold at 397B (41\% against
10\%) and 27B (33\% against 10\%), and thirtyfold at 4B, which earns 0.5\% of
the clue half --- and the halves correlate only weakly per game (Spearman
$\rho = +0.12 / +0.40 / -0.21$ for 397B / 27B / 4B over the 66 final answers
per tier).
Scored on clue recall alone, the smallest tier would sit at the floor and the
other two would read as near-total failures. The 70/30 weighting is a design
choice.

\paragraph{Controlling for round position removes the smallest tier's
response.} Uncontrolled, every tier appears to answer feedback; controlled,
the smallest does not. For each
round $t$ the Oracle's answer is paired with $s_{t+1}$, the stride into the next
question; ``irrelevant'' rounds are excluded. Confirmation arrives earlier than
refutation (mean round 12.1 / 9.7 / 10.9 for a ``yes'' against 15.6 / 15.6 / 15.9 for a ``no'') and early strides are larger, so an uncontrolled comparison is confounded
by position. Pairs are formed inside (puzzle, round-bin) cells with bins
$\{1$--$5, 6$--$10, 11$--$15, 16$--$20, 21$--$25, 26$--$29\}$, and 95\% CIs
bootstrap over puzzles (4{,}000 resamples). Uncontrolled, all three tiers appear
to respond ($+0.024$ $[+0.004, +0.044]$; $+0.043$ $[+0.015, +0.070]$; $+0.034$
$[+0.023, +0.046]$); controlled, the smallest does not ($+0.001$ $[-0.009,
+0.011]$; $+0.028$ $[+0.010, +0.046]$; $+0.009$ $[+0.002, +0.017]$).

\paragraph{Search behaviour does not track puzzle structure.} How open a
puzzle is changes what an agent knows at round 1, but not how it searches
afterwards. Regressing each
per-trace indicator on under-determination, seeds averaged, 5{,}000
permutations: stride, late stride, per-round change in distance to each
anchor, mean anchor distance and rounds played are all n.s.\ at every tier ($|\rho| \leq 0.32$, $p > 0.10$).
Commit rate is the one indicator that leans: negative at all three tiers
($-0.32 / -0.25 / -0.43$), so more open puzzles draw fewer commitments. But
pooled $p = 0.13$, and 1 of 24 tests reaching $p < 0.05$ is the chance
expectation, so we report it as unresolved rather than as an effect.

\paragraph{Trajectory geometry does not predict the score.} Every trajectory
measure is flat against accuracy, and the intervals are narrow enough to bound
the effect rather than merely fail to find one. Best checkpoint accuracy per
game; Gaussian LMM, puzzle random intercept (the same 22 puzzles are replayed
by every tier, so games are not independent), tier fixed effect, ML fits,
likelihood-ratio tests against the covariate-only model. Stride
$\beta = -0.010$ per SD, 95\% CI $[-0.035, 0.014]$, $p = 0.42$; per-round
change in distance to the solution-aware anchor $\beta = -0.011$,
$[-0.035, 0.014]$, $p = 0.39$; stride $\times$ tier $p = 0.20$; the same for
the surface-only anchor $\beta = -0.020$, $[-0.044, 0.003]$, $p = 0.097$, the
closest any measure comes and still not claimed. Figure~\ref{fig:e3} shows the underlying per-trace geometry.

\paragraph{The Oracle passed a pre-deployment audit.} The deployed
DeepSeek-V3.1 scored 20/21 (95\%) on yes/no probes and 3/4 exact on
``irrelevant''; candidates below the 90\% bar on the yes/no items, or from the
Questioners' own family, were rejected before any agent was run. The audit
probes a candidate with 25 hand-crafted (question, expected-answer) pairs over
three puzzles, 21 with a definite yes/no answer and 4 expecting ``irrelevant''.
Model-call settings are given in \S\ref{sec:framework}.

\paragraph{Game budget.} Exhausting the 50{,}000-token game budget forces a
final answer and is recorded as the game outcome \texttt{token\_budget}.

\paragraph{Trajectory and difficulty pipeline.} The under-determination
covariate is scored independently of the outcome apparatus: it prompts
GLM-5.3-flash (cross-family with all Questioners) for the twelve cold guesses
and scores closeness as embedding similarity between guess and solution under
the same encoder, not with the composite score. The embedding pipeline is
specified in \S\ref{sec:framework}; two further details matter for
reproduction. Keyword extraction strips Latin characters and digits before
jieba segmentation, and late-game stride denotes the second half of a game's
strides.

\paragraph{Artifacts.} The harness, the puzzle set with per-item source
records, per-game logs including every checkpoint story, and the analysis
script behind each reported number are released in a public repository,
withheld here for double-blind review. Puzzle text comes from a public
community repertoire and is redistributed with attribution and a content
flag.

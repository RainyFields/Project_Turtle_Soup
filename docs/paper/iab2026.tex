\title{Narrowing Without Converging:\\ How Interactive LLM Agents Search Under Feedback}

\begin{abstract}
Interactive agents should use feedback to converge on correct answers, yet how
they search remains poorly understood. We study three Qwen model tiers on 22
Turtle Soup puzzles using scored answer checkpoints and embedded trajectories
of successive questions. Accuracy improves for only the first 10 of 30 rounds,
while semantic stride contracts four- to fivefold. Longer budgets, Oracle
quality, and puzzle under-determination do not explain this plateau. Feedback
also redirects search poorly: after refutations, all three tiers move toward
regions already ruled out. Agents can also reach solution-grade stories and
subsequently abandon them. These results identify two distinct failures,
ineffective feedback use and failure to preserve strong hypotheses, that
endpoint accuracy cannot distinguish. We release the evaluation harness,
process measures, verified puzzle set, and per-game logs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider a Turtle Soup puzzle: \emph{A man's body lies in the desert,
clutching half a matchstick; luggage and clothing are scattered around him.}
The solver sees only this surface and must recover the hidden explanation
through yes/no questions. In the solution, a hot-air balloon was losing
altitude. After discarding their luggage, the passengers drew lots, and the
man holding the short matchstick was thrown overboard.

Existing evaluations can determine whether an agent eventually recovers this
story, but not how it searches. The same endpoint score may describe an agent
that never leaves its initial hypothesis and one that discovers the answer but
later abandons it. Existing Turtle Soup benchmarks emphasize outcomes rather
than search dynamics (Appendix~\ref{app:rw})~\cite{turtlebench,splat,tsbench}.
This leaves a central question unresolved: how does feedback change an agent's
next move?

Turtle Soup provides a controlled setting for answering this question. The
interaction is textual, the Oracle supplies a discrete yes/no/irrelevant
signal, and each puzzle is deliberately under-determined. We introduce two
process-level probes. After every Oracle response, the Questioner produces a
complete checkpoint reconstruction, allowing accuracy to be tracked across
rounds. We also embed each question to trace the association trajectory and
measure its per-round semantic stride. We evaluate three Qwen model tiers on
22 human-played puzzles.

We test three hypotheses. \textbf{H1}: refutations progressively constrain
the hypothesis space, so shorter strides should accompany convergence.
\textbf{H2}: feedback redirects the next question, with refutations pushing
the search away and confirmations promoting local refinement. \textbf{H3}:
once an agent reaches a solution-grade reconstruction, it preserves and
commits that answer.

Our analysis shows that interactive search can narrow without converging. It
also separates two failures hidden by endpoint accuracy: circling around an
early hypothesis and abandoning a stronger earlier reconstruction. Finally,
we establish an important measurement boundary: embedding geometry can
describe these trajectories but cannot reliably score their success. We
release the evaluation harness, process instruments, verified puzzle set, and
per-game logs.

\section{Framework and experiments}\label{sec:framework}

\paragraph{Task and harness.}
A \textbf{Questioner} sees only the puzzle's visible \emph{surface} and asks
one yes/no question per round, following the SPLAT player--judge
protocol~\cite{splat}. An \textbf{Oracle} holds the hidden story and answers
yes, no, or irrelevant. The Questioner may submit a final story at any round
and must do so when the round budget is reached. The open-source harness
records every exchange and the seed for each game.

\paragraph{Checkpoint and commitment instrumentation.}\label{sec:checkpoint}
In E1, the Questioner writes its best complete reconstruction after every
Oracle response. We score each checkpoint, so every point on the accuracy
curve represents a full answer based on the evidence available at that round.
We separately record \emph{commitment}, defined by whether and when the
Questioner voluntarily submits a final story. Together, these measures
convert an endpoint benchmark into a scored process.

\paragraph{Puzzles.}
To ensure that the intended explanations are demonstrably recoverable, we
restrict evaluation to puzzles with a public record of human play. The
resulting set contains 22 puzzles from a Chinese Turtle Soup community site,
each retaining its source URL. Surfaces contain 9--108 characters, solutions
contain 13--200 characters, and the set includes 97 annotated key clues. The
repository separates this verified family from a quarantined generated
family, and automated tests prevent the two from being mixed.

\paragraph{Puzzle under-determination.}
Each puzzle receives an \textbf{under-determination} score that measures how
far the surface alone leaves a solver from the hidden solution. We generate
twelve complete stories from the surface without Oracle feedback and identify
the guess closest to the solution in embedding space. Under-determination
equals one minus this maximum similarity, with higher values indicating a
larger initial gap. We use the score as a puzzle-level covariate;
Appendix~\ref{app:set} provides its distribution and validation.

\paragraph{Outcome scoring.}
The outcome score combines \textbf{clue recall} (70 points) and
\textbf{causal-logic identity} (30 points), as detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}. The logic component recognizes causally
equivalent answers expressed through different wording. The clue component
anchors answers to the annotated evidence and prevents semantically distant
alternatives from receiving high scores.

\paragraph{Association-trajectory instrumentation.}\label{sec:traj}
Our second instrument analyzes the questions themselves, adapting forward
flow~\cite{forwardflow} and the Divergent Association Task~\cite{dat} from
human psychometrics. For each round, we extract keywords with jieba, embed
them using bge-small-zh-v1.5, and average the normalized embeddings into a
unit vector $q_t$. All distances are cosine distances.

We use two anchors. The surface-only anchor represents terms from the visible
puzzle surface, whereas the solution-aware anchor combines surface, solution,
and clue terms. The primary measures are the \textbf{stride},
$s_t=d(q_t,q_{t-1})$, and the \textbf{anchor distance},
$h_t=d(q_t,\mathcal{A})$, computed separately for each anchor. H1 predicts
that contraction in stride should accompany movement toward the solution and
improved checkpoint accuracy. H2 is tested by pairing each Oracle response at
round $t$ with the next stride $s_{t+1}$ and by comparing movement toward
previously confirmed and refuted regions. H3 is tested by comparing
solution-grade checkpoint peaks with subsequent commitment and final answers.
The lexical \texttt{question\_novelty} metric serves as an ablation that
cannot capture movement expressed through synonyms.

\paragraph{Experimental designs.}
E1 plays each game for up to 30 rounds and scores a checkpoint after every
round. E2 imposes hard caps of $\{5,10,15,20,25,30\}$ rounds and scores only
the forced final answer, providing a no-checkpoint control. E3 analyzes the
E1 trajectories using $\{s_t\}$, $\{h_t\}$, response-conditioned movement,
and checkpoint commitment. The keyword extractor is fixed across models,
preventing any model from being evaluated through its own vocabulary.

\paragraph{Model grid and validity controls.}
We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B on 22 puzzles with
three seeds. E1 therefore contains 198 thirty-round games, while E2 contains
1,188 games across the six caps. DeepSeek-V3.1 serves as both Oracle and
causal-logic judge. It is cross-family with every Questioner and achieves
95\% accuracy on held-out yes/no probes. Every model call uses
\texttt{max\_tokens}=2048, disabled thinking, temperature 0.2, and a
50,000-token per-game budget. Analyses isolate the documented
token-exhaustion artifact affecting the smallest tier; no harness failures
occurred. Because the Questioners differ in training generation and
architecture, we compare model tiers rather than claim a scaling law.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Interaction improves accuracy only during the first ten rounds.}\label{sec:e1}
Checkpoint accuracy increased through approximately round 10 and then
plateaued at every tier (Figure~\ref{fig:e1fig}a). From rounds 10 to 30,
accuracy changed by $-0.009$, $+0.003$, and $-0.013$ for 4B, 27B, and 397B,
respectively; all confidence intervals included zero. Each tier also ended
below its best checkpoint accuracy (Figure~\ref{fig:e1fig}b).

The plateau was not explained by the round budget. Final accuracy was
insensitive to the cap for the two larger tiers. The apparent decline at 4B
coincided with token exhaustion, which rose from 0/66 to 63/66 games across
caps; no decline appeared among non-exhausted games. The Oracle achieved
95\% accuracy on held-out probes, and 68--74\% of its responses were ``no'',
ruling out a collapsed feedback channel. The rank correlations between
under-determination and round-1 accuracy were $-0.34/-0.54/-0.47$. No
relationship was detected with improvement after round 10, any trajectory
measure, either score component, or the between-tier gap (all n.s.\ at
$n=22$; Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\paragraph{H1: the search narrows toward the answer.}\label{sec:h1}
Mean stride decreased monotonically at every tier, falling four- to fivefold
from $0.145/0.232/0.182$ at round 1 to $0.025/0.053/0.045$ at round 29. This
contraction did not accompany convergence: accuracy remained flat, and
within-tier stride did not predict final performance ($|\rho|\leq0.16$). The
4B tier had the smallest late-game stride, $0.030$ $[0.021,0.039]$, compared
with $0.082$ $[0.065,0.098]$ for 27B, yet never reached a solution-grade
answer. Its trajectory therefore contracted around a persistent hypothesis
rather than toward the solution. Excluding its 17\% truncated or
non-interrogative questions changed the mean and late stride only from
$0.049$ and $0.030$ to $0.056$ and $0.033$, respectively.

\paragraph{H2: the answer redirects the next question.}\label{sec:feedback}
We paired each Oracle response with the next stride and controlled for round
position within puzzle and round-bin cells. Let $\Delta s$ denote stride
after confirmation minus stride after refutation. No difference was detected
for 4B ($+0.001$ $[-0.009,+0.011]$), whereas 27B and 397B moved farther after
confirmation ($+0.028$ $[+0.010,+0.046]$ and $+0.009$ $[+0.002,+0.017]$;
Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}a). Response-conditioned movement was therefore
absent at the smallest tier and opposite to the hypothesized direction at the
larger tiers.

Because an informative confirmation could reasonably produce a larger step,
we also used a direction-based test that assumed no optimal stride. After a
refutation, the next question moved toward regions the Oracle had already
ruled out. This effect appeared at every tier: $-0.089$ $[-0.112,-0.068]$
for 4B, $-0.051$ $[-0.075,-0.032]$ for 27B, and $-0.032$ $[-0.047,-0.018]$
for 397B. No corresponding asymmetry appeared after confirmation. Feedback
therefore failed to steer any tier consistently away from rejected regions.

\paragraph{H3: the agent stops when it finds the answer.}\label{sec:patterns}
We defined a sustained checkpoint peak of at least $0.5$ as
\emph{solution-grade}. The 4B tier reached no solution-grade checkpoint. The
27B tier reached solution-grade checkpoints in four games and voluntarily
committed in three; 397B did so in six games but committed in only one
(Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}b). Commitment was rare and late overall,
occurring in 11/66, 9/66, and 2/66 games at mean rounds 11.1, 14.2, and
23.5.

The dominant failure differed by tier. The 4B tier circled around an early
hypothesis in 39/66 games and abandoned a stronger answer in one. The 397B
tier circled in 6/66 games and abandoned a stronger answer in 12. The 397B
tier therefore reached useful hypotheses more often but did not reliably
preserve or commit them.

\paragraph{Embedding geometry describes abandonment but does not score it.}
In abandoning games, the median cosine similarity between the final and peak
stories was $0.851$. This was comparable to the similarity between
checkpoints from the same game ($0.841$) and much greater than that between
stories from different puzzles ($0.498$; Figure~\ref{fig:feedback}c).
Despite this semantic proximity, abandoning the peak reduced the score by
$0.30$ on average. The decisive substitution was therefore smaller than the
encoder's resolution.

Consistent with this limit, no systematic drift was detected, and all
within-tier coefficients linking stride or drift to best accuracy were
bounded within $\pm0.035$ per standard deviation
(Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}). Lexical novelty detected only the 4B tier's
repeated questions. The trajectory geometry characterized how agents failed,
but it did not predict how well they scored.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

\textbf{Limitations.}
Our evidence comes from one game, one Oracle protocol, and three Qwen-family
Questioners. The trajectory measures capture how far each question moves in
embedding space, not what operation it performs; round position is
controlled only by binning, the anchors are not human-derived, and a
single encoder may miss small semantic changes that alter correctness. Token
exhaustion can mimic inability to form questions; the Oracle's 95\%
audited accuracy implies roughly one wrong response per game; and using the
same DeepSeek model as Oracle and logic judge may correlate their errors.
Under-determination may partly measure puzzle familiarity, though no claim
rests on it. The puzzles include death and other dark themes and
carry content flags.

\textbf{Conclusion.}
Here, the agents narrow without converging:
semantic stride decreases four- to fivefold while accuracy plateaus, and
feedback fails to steer questions consistently away from rejected regions.
The smallest tier mostly circles early hypotheses, whereas the largest tier
sometimes reaches solution-grade reconstructions but fails to preserve or
commit them. The two failures require different fixes yet appear
identical under endpoint accuracy; process-level measurement reveals how
search fails even when it cannot predict performance. Whether these patterns extend to other tasks, model families, and protocols
remains open.

\section{Related work}\label{app:rw}
Existing turtle-soup benchmarks, TurtleBench~\cite{turtlebench},
SPLAT~\cite{splat} (whose interactive player--judge protocol we adopt), and
TurtleSoup-Bench~\cite{tsbench}, score verification accuracy, solve
efficiency, or faithfulness to the intended story. TurtleSoup-Bench probes
what agents ask next but grades it against the intended story; none of the
three measures the search's geometry, which is our delta.
MUTATE~\cite{mutate} shows frontier agents find far fewer mechanism-distinct
paths than humans; its diverge-then-narrow agent is a natural test
intervention for our metrics. The instrument itself adapts forward
flow~\cite{forwardflow} and the Divergent Association Task~\cite{dat} from
human psychometrics. The Small World of Words norms~\cite{swow} provide the
human anchor that ours is not yet (\S\ref{sec:traj}). Judge-bias
catalogues~\cite{calm,posbias} shape the scoring: the model judge is confined
to the one subscore that objective matching cannot reach
(Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}).

\section{The puzzle set}\label{app:set}

\textbf{Where the puzzles come from.} A puzzle nobody has solved carries no
evidence that its clues suffice, that its solution is unique, or that the
intended leap is reachable; an agent scoring zero on it teaches us nothing. Our
set is hand-picked
from the classic repertoire of a Chinese Turtle Soup community: 22 puzzles people have
played and solved, each keeping its source record. Every item was read and kept or
rejected by hand; rejected were puzzles turning on deduction from stated evidence
rather than a lateral reframing, and puzzles whose surfaces were incomplete at the
source. Surfaces run 9--108 characters (median 34), solutions 13--200 (median 102).

\textbf{How hard are they?} Each puzzle carries an \textbf{under-determination} score:
give a model the surface alone, with no Oracle and no feedback, let it write
twelve complete stories, and take the closest to the real solution. The index is
one minus that best guess, and is large when the surface leaves a solver far
from the answer.

The median is 0.347, quartiles 0.273 and 0.410, full range 0.173--0.577:
single-peaked with a thin hard tail. Easiest are the story the genre is named for (0.17)
and the desert matchstick (0.19). Hardest is a fourteen-character surface
reading \emph{our heights differ / a flowerpot broke / our heights are the same}
(0.58).

Two properties make this usable as a covariate. It describes the puzzle, not any
agent's performance on it. It is also independent of rounds used; a difficulty
defined as ``how many rounds this takes'' would make an accuracy-versus-rounds
plot confirm itself. Nor is it a proxy for surface length ($r=-0.22$, n.s.), so
length dependence was not detected at $n = 22$ (a weak test). It is one measure rather
than two: a surface loose enough to admit many mechanisms is, for that same
reason, one whose true mechanism takes more reframing to reach, so \emph{how
far} and \emph{how many ways} are not separable here.

\textbf{A caveat we report either way.} The two puzzles scoring easiest are
among the most widely circulated, and the models' own first-round guesses are
consistent with familiarity contributing: on the best-known of them the two
larger tiers open at 0.61 and 0.53 before any evidence arrives, against 0.24 and
0.36 on a puzzle of almost identical under-determination. The measure may
therefore read familiarity as much as difficulty on the easy end. Separating the
two calls for a memorisation probe, which we have not run; nothing in this paper
rests on the measure.

\section{Measuring agent performance}\label{app:metrics}



\textbf{What counts as a correct answer.} Judged outcome is \textbf{clue recall (70)}
plus \textbf{causal-logic identity (30)}. Clue recall matches the answer against
per-puzzle annotated key clues: objective and reproducible. The logic judge
rates only whether cause $\to$ mechanism $\to$ outcome matches and is told
explicitly to ignore wording. Because single ratings are unstable on borderline
answers, ratings are sampled and averaged (two samples per rating in the
reported grid). The gap between the two subscores is itself readable: right
story, wrong vocabulary.

Clue matching tolerates paraphrase through character-bigram recall, which sets a
floor on how short an annotated clue may be. Below that floor only a verbatim
match counts, and the score reads vocabulary rather than content. Clues are
annotated above the floor.

\textbf{Does the logic half earn its thirty points?} On the full grid, yes:
every model earns proportionally far more of the judged causal-logic half than
of the clue-recall half (397B averages 12.6/30 logic against 7.2/70 clues, 27B
10.1 against 6.8, 4B 2.9 against 0.2), and the halves correlate only weakly
per game (Spearman $\rho = 0.30 / 0.35 / 0.06$ for 397B / 27B / 4B over 396
E2 games each): models reach the right causal shape well before, and often
without, landing the annotated clue content. The 70/30 weighting itself is a
design choice; every conclusion in \S\ref{sec:results} is readable from the
two halves separately, though the absolute plateau level (${\sim}0.2$) does
depend on the weights. The logic judge rates the causal-logic half on its
30-point scale and is sampled twice per rating at temperature 0.2; the two
samples agree exactly in 86\% of ratings and differ by under one point of 30
on average.

\textbf{Feedback response: definition, confound, and both estimates.} For each
round $t$ the Oracle's answer is paired with $s_{t+1}$, the stride into the next
question; ``irrelevant'' rounds are excluded. Confirmation arrives earlier than
refutation (mean round 12.1 / 9.7 / 10.9 for a ``yes'' against 15.6 / 15.6 / 15.9 for a ``no'') and early strides are larger, so an uncontrolled comparison is confounded
by position. Pairs are formed inside (puzzle, round-bin) cells with bins
$\{1$--$5, 6$--$10, 11$--$15, 16$--$20, 21$--$25, 26$--$29\}$, and 95\% CIs
bootstrap over puzzles (4{,}000 resamples). Uncontrolled, all three tiers appear
to respond ($+0.024$ $[+0.004, +0.044]$; $+0.043$ $[+0.015, +0.070]$; $+0.034$
$[+0.023, +0.046]$); controlled, the smallest does not ($+0.001$ $[-0.009,
+0.011]$; $+0.028$ $[+0.010, +0.046]$; $+0.009$ $[+0.002, +0.017]$). The gap
between the two is the point.

\textbf{The semantic scale of abandoning.} Within abandoning games (sustained
peak $\geq 0.3$, final below half of it; $n = 34$), the final checkpoint story
sits at cosine 0.851 (median) from the peak story, while any two checkpoints of
the same game sit at 0.841 (median over all 77{,}906 within-game pairs from 60
games) and two stories from different puzzles at 0.498 (231 pairs). The score
gap across that same pair is $0.447 \rightarrow 0.099$.

\textbf{The round-1 prior.} The round-1 checkpoint precedes the first Oracle
answer and is therefore a cold guess. It holds 49\% / 33\% / 26\% (median, by
tier) of the game's eventual best score, and correlates with it at $\rho =
+0.37 / +0.57 / +0.47$ (permutation $p = 0.09 / 0.003 / 0.03$, $n = 22$ puzzles
with seeds averaged). Under-determination, itself measured from twelve cold
guesses by a model cross-family with every Questioner, predicts round-1 quality
at $\rho = -0.34 / -0.54 / -0.47$ ($p = 0.12 / 0.010 / 0.029$) but not the final
score.

\textbf{Behavioural indicators against puzzle structure.} Regressing each
per-trace indicator on under-determination, seeds averaged, 5{,}000
permutations: stride, late stride, drift (both anchors), mean anchor distance
and rounds played are all n.s.\ at every tier ($|\rho| \leq 0.32$, $p > 0.10$).
Commit rate is negative at all three ($-0.32 / -0.25 / -0.43$) but pooled $p =
0.13$, and 1 of 24 tests reaching $p < 0.05$ is the chance expectation, so we
report it as unresolved rather than as an effect.

\textbf{Retention estimator and permutation null.} Retention is the mean
per-game final-to-sustained-peak ratio over games with a nonzero sustained
peak; the final is the last played checkpoint, and excluding early-committing
games changes each tier's value by $\leq 0.03$ (bootstrap 95\% CIs
$\pm{\approx}0.08$). For the null: unit, the game. For each of 2{,}000
permutations, every game's checkpoint sequence is shuffled within the game
(knowledge-constant, jitter-only null), the sustained peak and the final
(last element) are recomputed on the shuffled sequence, and retention is the
mean over that tier's games with a nonzero permuted peak. The reported $p$ is
one-sided per tier, the fraction of permutations whose null retention is at
or below the observed value. Observed retention (0.69--0.76 by tier) does not
fall significantly below this null (0.79--0.84, $p \geq 0.09$), so the
peak-to-final gap visible in Figure~\ref{fig:e1fig}b is reported as consistent
with scoring jitter and no claim in the body rests on its size; the commitment
record of \S\ref{sec:patterns} is behavioural and unaffected by jitter.
Late-game stride means the second half of a game's strides.

\textbf{Mixed-model coefficients.} Best checkpoint accuracy per game; Gaussian
LMM, puzzle random intercept, tier fixed effect, ML fits, likelihood-ratio
tests against the covariate-only model. Stride $\beta = -0.010$ per SD, 95\%
CI $[-0.035, 0.014]$, $p = 0.42$; drift $\beta = -0.011$, $[-0.035, 0.014]$,
$p = 0.39$; stride $\times$ tier $p = 0.20$; surface-anchor drift
$\beta = -0.020$, $[-0.044, 0.003]$, $p = 0.097$.

\textbf{Environment settings and Oracle audit.} Every model call uses
max\_tokens 2048 (thinking disabled, temperature 0.2) with a per-game budget
of 50{,}000 tokens; exhausting the game budget forces a final answer and is
recorded as the game outcome \texttt{token\_budget}. The Oracle audit probes a
candidate with 25 hand-crafted (question, expected-answer) pairs over three
puzzles, 21 with a definite yes/no answer and 4 expecting ``irrelevant''; the
pass bar is 90\% on the yes/no items. The deployed DeepSeek-V3.1 scored 20/21
(95\%) on yes/no and 3/4 exact on ``irrelevant''. Candidates measured in the
pilot phase: a 4B Oracle with thinking disabled, 30\% (rejected; in that pilot
configuration, raising Oracle accuracy from
30\% to 100\% left the 4B Questioner's score unchanged); the same model with
thinking enabled, 70\% (rejected); a same-family flash model, 90\% (excluded
for sharing the Questioners' family); GLM-5.3-flash, 100\% (used in earlier
tooling).

\textbf{Trajectory and difficulty pipeline.} Keywords are extracted with
jieba after stripping Latin characters and digits; each keyword is embedded
with bge-small-zh-v1.5 (normalized), and the round vector is the re-normalized
mean of its keyword embeddings. All distances are cosine. The solution-aware
anchor embeds the puzzle's surface, solution, and clue terms; the surface-only
anchor embeds surface terms alone. The under-determination measure prompts
GLM-5.3-flash (cross-family with all Questioners) for twelve cold guesses from
the surface and scores closeness as embedding similarity between guess and
solution under the same encoder, not with the composite score, so the
covariate is not coupled to the outcome apparatus.

\textbf{Artifacts.} Harness, puzzle set with per-item source records, per-game
logs (including every checkpoint story), and the analysis scripts behind each
reported number are released at
a public repository, withheld here for double-blind review and available to
reviewers on request through the programme chairs. Puzzle surfaces and
solutions originate from a public community repertoire and are redistributed
with source attribution and a content flag.

\textbf{Where the accuracy curve comes from.} E1 does not score once at the end.
Every round, after the Oracle answers, the Questioner is required to commit its
best complete story, and \emph{that} is scored. Each point on the accuracy
curve is therefore a full answer written with the evidence available at that
round.

Three things the table shows that no mean curve can. \textbf{The checkpoint
mechanism surfaces knowledge the agent never acts on.} The round-10 committed
story \emph{is} the solution, synthesized from earlier answers on a round whose
own question was answered ``no''. Yet the model never volunteers a final answer in
all thirty rounds (one of the 57/66 non-committing 397B games), and by round 19
it has abandoned the correct reading for a falling-coverings mechanism it never
escapes. \textbf{Stalling in fresh words happens at the largest scale too.}
Rounds 22--30 are eight lexically distinct re-asks of the same flooding
hypothesis, exactly the pattern lexical novelty misses. \textbf{The curve's
round-to-round jitter is a scoring fact, not a knowledge fact.} The same
understanding re-worded (rounds 3~$\to$~4) swings the clue half $70 \to 0$. The
mean curves of \S\ref{sec:results} average this out, which is why single-game
curves are not the unit of analysis.
